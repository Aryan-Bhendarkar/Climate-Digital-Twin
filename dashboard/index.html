        How it works

        • The model. Three trained copies, averaged. Each is a small fully-convolutional neural emulator steps India's weather one day at a time (t2m, rain, u10, v10, msl, tcwv on a 32×36, 1° grid), rolled forward autoregressively for 10 days from the last 3 observed days.
        • Anomalies, not raw fields. It predicts the 1-day change of the anomaly from a smooth seasonal climatology (a few Fourier harmonics per cell, stored in the model weights). Rain is handled in log space so it cannot go negative.
        • Local by construction. Each step stacks 8 blocks with a single 3×3 convolution, so information can travel at most 8 cells per day. A shock therefore starts near where it is applied and spreads outward — the Ripple tab measures exactly this.
        • Multi-step training. After 1-step training it is fine-tuned on unrolled multi-day rollouts so it learns to correct its own errors and not drift or blur.
        • Multiverse. “Run shock” adds your physical delta to the last input day, then calls the same model twice in parallel: Prime (unperturbed) and Multiverse. Δ is Multiverse − Prime. A null shock must be bit-identical.
        • Impact. The Kharif Stress Monitor and wind index are computed in your browser from those two live rollouts. Rain departure bands follow IMD conventions but measure departure from Prime; heat-stress uses per-state monthly P95 of daily-mean temperature (training-period climate statistic); they are indicative, not forecasts. State boundaries: Natural Earth (public domain), indicative only.
        • Limits. It is a statistical emulator, not a dynamical core: skill fades with lead time, heavy rain is hard, and shocks far outside the training range are extrapolation.
